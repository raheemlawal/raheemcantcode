## The problem

Trucking contractors run payroll on spreadsheets, and the pay structure is
genuinely complicated. Drivers are paid per mile, per trip, per day or hourly,
often several of those at once, with bonuses above mileage thresholds and splits
between team drivers. On top of that, every pay change in a spreadsheet is
retroactive by accident, because a spreadsheet has no concept of when a rate
started.

## What it does

A web app for fleet operations: dispatch, vehicles, maintenance, inspections,
fuel, compliance, and the part that carries the most weight, payroll.
Settlements come in, driver pay is computed against each driver's terms, and
paystubs and notifications go out. Drivers get their own portal for pay history,
documents and time off.

## How it works

**Effective-dated rates as a whole set, not per column.** A raise is a new row
carrying the driver's entire rate set with an inclusive start and end date, not
an update to a column. "What was this driver paid under on this date" becomes a
single lookup, instead of resolving each column independently and risking an
answer that straddles two changes and describes a rate combination that never
existed. Overlapping windows for one driver are made impossible by an exclusion
constraint in the database, not by a check in application code.

**Payroll resolves as of the settlement date; display reads the current value.**
The drivers table kept its rate columns, as the current value for display and as
the fallback for anyone with no rate rows at all. So adding effective dating
changed nothing for any driver who had not used it yet. A nightly job rolls that
denormalised value forward when a scheduled change becomes current, and it
touches only display, never what is paid.

**Rates travel as JSON, not twenty positional arguments.** The set will grow. A
wide function signature has to be dropped and recreated every time it does,
which had already happened to a different function in this codebase for exactly
that reason. Known-key reads off a JSON payload survive the next four fields.

**Authorisation lives in the database, not the interface.** Writes go through
security-definer functions that check permissions themselves; the UI's
permission check only decides what to render. The useful consequence shows up in
the driver portal: a driver reading their own pay history sees past and current
windows but not future scheduled ones. A raise the office has not announced yet
is not the function's to disclose, and if that rule lived in the UI it would be
one request away from being bypassed.

**Immutable history for anything that touches money.** Time-off grants, usage,
cancellations, adjustments, carryover and expiry are all rows, never overwrites.
Cancelling approved time off restores the balance and preserves the original
request. The moment a past week's pay can be silently recomputed, the records
stop being evidence of what happened.

## The hardest part

A feature that looked finished and was not.

The compensation rules table had effective-start and effective-end columns and
had read as implemented for a long time. The function that computes payroll
never referenced either one. Dated rules silently applied to every week,
including weeks before they existed. Nobody had caught it because the presence
of the columns was doing a convincing impression of the feature working.

The verification mattered more than the fix. This change touched the function
that decides what every driver is paid, so "it looks right" was not a standard I
could ship against. I fingerprinted the payroll preview output for every
settlement in the system, hashing the driver rows, before touching anything.
Then I backfilled every driver with their current values, effective from a date
earlier than the oldest trip in the system, and re-ran it. All 41 settlements
came back byte-identical, which was the only acceptable result: new machinery,
zero change to historical pay. Then the positive test, splitting one driver's
window at a date with a raise and confirming the old rate resolved the day
before and the new one the day after, with past settlements untouched.

The first attempt at the resolver failed to compile on a reference to a driver's
first name, and that error turned out to be the valuable part. It meant the
resolver had to return *every* column the calling functions read off their driver
alias, not just the pay terms. Following that thread surfaced four more pay
terms the plan had missed entirely. Shipping without them would have meant a
raise taking effect only partly on its effective date, which is worse than
having no effective dating at all. One is visibly absent; the other is quietly
wrong.

## Outcome

Live, and used for payroll every week. Shipped since: configurable time-off and
holiday pay with a grant engine, recurring payroll adjustments, metered
subscription billing with single-use pilot codes, a dashboard of operational
widgets, and the marketing site itself, moved off a hosted page builder onto its
own deploy.
